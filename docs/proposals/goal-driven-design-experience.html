01

The distinction everything rests on

Constraints are not objectives

“Span under a metre” and “as efficient as possible” are different kinds of statement, and a tool that puts them on the same kind of slider will produce nonsense.

KindWhat it doesExampleWidget
Constraint Partitions the space into feasible and infeasible. Violating it makes a design invalid, not worse Span ≤ 1.0 m · t/c ≥ 9% for structure A limit with a feasible-space readout
Objective Orders the feasible designs Maximise L/D at cruise A priority weight with a cost-of-moving readout
Requirement An objective with a threshold. Behaves as a constraint until met, an objective after Flying by 8 kn A target marker on the objective's own axis
Context Not a design variable at all — it defines the problem Rider 95 kg · seawater 15 °C A plain input. Changing it re-poses the problem

Why this matters concretely. A solver that treats span as an objective will happily return a 1.4 m wing that is 3% more efficient and unusable. A solver that treats rider weight as a design variable will “optimise” by making the rider lighter. Both are real failure modes of naive slider UIs, and both are prevented by typing the inputs.

The fourth category is the one usually missed. Rider weight, water type and wind range are context. When the user drags rider weight from 75 to 95 kg they are not adjusting the design — they are asking a different question, and every result on screen becomes stale. The UI must show that, not silently re-rank.

02

Why this is possible at all

Ten microseconds is the whole argument

The closed-form estimation chain evaluates a candidate wing in about 10 µs. That single number decides which experiences are available.

TierCostCandidates per 100 msWhat the interface can do
Estimator~10 µs~10,000 Re-optimise while the slider is still moving. The Pareto front updates live
Panel / VLM10–100 ms1–10 Re-evaluate the current design on release; optimise in a background job
OpenFOAM / SU2minutes–hours— Verify a chosen design. Never in a loop

A population of 200 over 100 generations is 20,000 evaluations — 0.2 seconds. The search is free. That inverts the usual engineering problem: the hard part is not finding good designs, it is helping someone decide which good design they want.

This is also the strongest evidence for the layered architecture. A tool built directly on CFD cannot offer this experience at any price.

Precedent, and it is close. An interactive framework pairing Athena Vortex Lattice — a low-fidelity solver at the same tier as our VLM — with interactive multi-objective particle swarm optimisation was demonstrated for aerodynamic shape design, with the decision maker periodically supplying preference information during the optimisation to steer the search. Two things are validated at once: a cheap solver in the loop, and preference expressed during the run rather than declared up front.

03

The experience

Four panes, one live model

Wing Designer — racing wing, wingfoil, 10–20 kn

What each pane is for

  1. The brief is prose, and it stays visible. It is not parsed into settings and then discarded — it is the record of intent that the constraints below are meant to encode, and the user should be able to see when the two have drifted apart.
  2. Constraints show remaining feasible space (“62% of design space still feasible”). This is the single most useful thing a constraint widget can display: it tells you when you have over-constrained the problem before the optimiser returns nothing.
  3. Objectives are priorities, not weights. High/medium/low is what a designer can actually express; 0.42 versus 0.38 is not. The optimiser converts.
  4. Context is visually separate and marks results stale when changed. It is a different kind of act.
  5. The candidate list is a Pareto set, not a ranking. Three designs that are each best at something. The trade-off scatter underneath shows why they all survive.
  6. The envelope chart keeps the previous design as a ghost line. Design is comparative; the question is always “is this better than what I had?”
04

Interaction

Three gestures, in order of value

1. Drag a constraint — watch feasibility, not the design

Tightening span from 1.0 m to 0.9 m should immediately show how much you gave up: the feasible fraction drops, the Pareto front retreats, and the best achievable L/D falls by a stated amount. The answer to “can I have a shorter span?” is a number, not a yes.

2. Brush the trade-off — the preference brush

Brushing is the standard gesture for filtering a design space. The refinement worth adopting is the preference brush: a range brush locked at the high-quality end of an axis, so it always contains the best solutions while removing the need to choose a threshold. The user says “more of this” without inventing a number.

3. Pin and compare

Design exploration is non-linear and people need to go back. MIT's Design Space Exploration toolset explicitly supports reconstructing previous designs, and that capability is worth copying directly. Every candidate ever shown is pinnable; the ghost line in the envelope chart is the pinned design.

What the tool must never do: silently move something the user set. If a constraint makes the objective unreachable, say so and show the closest feasible design — do not quietly relax the constraint to produce a result. This is the difference between a tool that is trusted and one that is second-guessed.

05

Under it

The solver, and why it is the easy part

Design vector

~6–8 planform numbers (area, aspect, taper, sweep, anhedral, washout) plus CST section coefficients. Small, continuous, and always valid by construction — the CST class function makes non-airfoils unrepresentable.

~14 dims
Constraints

Feasibility-first ranking: any feasible design beats any infeasible one; among infeasible, less violation wins. Keeps the population inside the space without penalty weights that need tuning.

4 hard
Search

NSGA-II or MOPSO over the estimator. Chosen not for sophistication but because at 10 µs per evaluation the entire search costs 0.2 s. Population-based, no gradients, native multi-objective.

~0.2 s
Verification

Estimator optima are candidates, never answers. Each finalist re-evaluated at the VLM tier before it is offered, and at CFD before it is machined.

10–100 ms

The real risk, stated plainly: optimisation amplifies model error. A search over 20,000 candidates will find precisely the corner of the design space where the estimator is most wrong, and present it as the winner. This is not a hypothetical — it is what optimisers do.

Three mitigations, all mandatory: (a) every optimum is re-checked at a higher tier before display; (b) the tool shows where a candidate sits relative to the estimator's validity envelope — attached flow, small α, high Re — and flags any that sit near the edge; (c) candidates are ranked with a confidence alongside the score, not by score alone.

06

Honest limits

What this cannot do yet

  • It cannot invent a section. It selects from the catalog and perturbs CST coefficients. True inverse design — naming a pressure distribution and getting a shape — is a different and much larger capability. The frontier work (3DID and similar) couples a learned latent representation with physics-aware optimisation, and needs a training corpus that does not exist for hydrofoils.
  • It cannot judge structure. Thickness is treated as a constraint with a hydrodynamic price, but nothing here computes whether a 9% section actually survives the loads. That is a real gap and the reason t/c must stay a user-set constraint rather than something optimised.
  • It optimises steady, attached flow. Pumping, take-off transients and manoeuvre are unsteady, and none of the tiers models them.
  • Multi-objective UIs can overwhelm. PAVED handles up to ten criteria, but “handles” is not “is useful”. Five objectives is a guess, and it should be tested with a real user before it is built out.

Scope note. The design object is a single wing — front or rear. A rear wing is still designed in the downwash of a front wing, so the surrounding assembly must be present as context that supplies the flow field. A stabiliser evaluated in free stream is evaluated wrong.
