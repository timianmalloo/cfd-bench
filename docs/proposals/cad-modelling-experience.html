01

The paradigm choice

Three ways to build CAD, and only one fits

ParadigmExemplarMental modelVerdict
Direct NURBS manipulationRhino No history tree. Curves, surfaces and solids are first-class; you push control points and manage continuity G0–G3 Half right The interaction model is right; unconstrained surface editing is not
Visual programmingGrasshopper Components wired on a canvas. Grew from Rutten's 2008 question — “what if you could have explicit control over the history?” Not v1 Large surface, and the generative layer already covers the parametric need
Relational geometryMultiSurf / SurfaceWorks A hierarchy of dependencies; change a parameter and dependents update The idea, not the format Its model is not NURBS, so it does not round-trip to other CAD — a trap we must avoid

The recommendation: direct manipulation of a constrained parametric model. The user does not want free-form NURBS editing — that is Rhino, and it would let them build something that is not a foil. They want to edit the outline, the anhedral, the chord, the thickness, the twist. Those are exactly the distribution curves already in the station model.

So: the editable objects are the distribution curves, never the surface. Editing “the outline” means editing the chord-versus-span curve. The surface is always derived. That single decision preserves two invariants at once — the geometry is always a valid foil, and every edit maps to parameters the estimator understands, so L/D can update while you drag.

The MultiSurf lesson is worth stating separately because it is the closest existing analogue — parametric relational surfaces for marine design — and its recorded weakness is precisely the one we must dodge: a native model that other CAD systems cannot read. Our model may be relational internally, but what leaves the tool must be NURBS/B-Rep.

02

The closest analogue

What Shape3d already got right

Shape3d designs surfboards from concept to CNC — a lofted marine surface edited through orthogonal curves. It is a closer analogue to a foil editor than any mechanical CAD package, and its interaction details are worth copying almost verbatim.

Shape3d behaviourWhy it matters for a foil
Left-click a control point to select (turns red); move with mouse or arrow keys, with accurate keyboard displacement steps Keyboard nudge is not optional. Precision work needs it; mouse-only editing is for sketching
Double-click on the curve adds a control point there; Delete or right-click removes one Control-point count is a design decision, not a fixed property. Fewer points = fairer curve
Multi-point selection and alignment Straightening a run of stations is a real operation
Hold left-click in outline/profile/thickness view to see the slice flow with width, rocker and thickness plus distance from each end simultaneously The most valuable idea here. Show derived quantities during the drag. Our version adds the one Shape3d cannot: live L/D
Display thickness between any two curves Direct analogue: t/c between upper and lower section surfaces

The multi-view paradigm transfers exactly. A surfboard is edited as outline + profile + thickness. A foil is edited as planform outline, anhedral, chord distribution, thickness distribution and twist distribution — five orthogonal curves that fully determine the surface.

The three decisions this screen encodes

  1. Curve tabs, not a feature tree. Five curves fully determine the surface, and each is a familiar 2D editing problem. A user who understands surfboard shaping already understands this.
  2. The curvature comb is always on. Curvature discontinuities are hydrodynamically real and visually invisible — the exact hazard flagged in the geometry knowledge base. At G1 the combs are parallel where they meet but of different lengths; at G2 they match. Degree-5 splines give G4 continuity internally against degree-3's G2, which is why degree 5 is the default for a surface whose pressure distribution is a derivative of its curvature.
  3. The live strip carries L/D and cavitation margin. This is the thing no existing CAD tool does and the whole reason to build rather than buy: the estimator runs on every drag, so the designer sees the hydrodynamic consequence of a control-point move at the moment they make it. The warning on tip αeff is the validity envelope enforcing itself.

The stack

Build three layers, buy four

NURBS surfaces, B-Rep, booleans, STEPBuy OCCT + OCC C# Wrapper Years of work. Do not attempt. And STEP export is what makes the tool manufacturable — see below
2D chartsBuyScottPlot Best .NET performance on large scientific datasets
Mesh / STL outputEither— Trivial from our own loft; OCCT also does it

The OCCT decision deserves to be made explicitly rather than drifted into. It is a large native dependency with a wrapper build step and a real learning curve. It earns its place for exactly one reason: STEP export. STL is not appropriate for CNC — tessellation discards tolerance and produces facets on the surface whose curvature is the entire point of the design. STEP needs a B-Rep kernel, and writing one is not a reasonable undertaking.

So the honest sequencing is: if v1 exports STL for meshing and visualisation only, OCCT is deferrable. It becomes required the moment someone wants to machine a mold. Decide by asking whether CAM is a v1 requirement, not by asking whether a kernel would be nice.

Where designs come from

Three doors into the editor

From the optimiser

A candidate from the goal-driven experience arrives as explicit stations. The generative layer burst into editable geometry — and the flow is one-way by design, so hand edits never fight the parameters that produced them.

primary
From a catalog

A discipline preset — downwind, race, surf — instantiated at the user's rider weight and speed range. The fastest path to a sane starting point.

easy
From a file

Opening an existing wing. This is the hard one and should be scoped honestly: importing an arbitrary STEP surface and back-fitting it to stations is a fitting problem, not a parsing problem, and it is substantially harder than export.

defer

A realistic middle path for import: accept section coordinates plus a planform table — formats we already read — rather than arbitrary B-Rep. That covers “open a wing someone described” without solving surface reconstruction.

06

Risks

What could make this harder than it looks

  • OCCT licensing. LGPL-family with exceptions. Load-bearing and unverified — it must be read before commitment, because it gates the STEP path and therefore the whole fabrication story.
  • Does HelixToolkit's gizmo generalise to control-point dragging? The demos show object transforms. Dragging one control point in a 3D view, constrained to a plane, is a different interaction. Worth a spike before committing.
  • Spline lofting can produce invisible defects. Interpolation through sparse stations can introduce curvature reversals that are real and unseen — which is why the comb is always on rather than a menu item.
  • Undo across a parametric model is not free. A control-point drag changes a curve, which regenerates the surface, which re-runs the estimator. Undo must restore the model, not the surface, and the granularity of a “drag” needs deciding early.
